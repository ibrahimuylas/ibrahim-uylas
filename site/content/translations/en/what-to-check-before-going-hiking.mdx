Knowing what *could* happen on a hike makes your hike safer, because even unintentionally, you’ll take some precautions. Just like a football team researches their opponent’s most dangerous aspects before a match. And I’ll now tell you a bit about what you should do for a relaxing and safe hike.

## Foot Alignment Technique

It means to keep your feet separate. Don’t walk by dragging one foot. Always be on a single foot. If you walk carefully, you will eventually realize that you are walking with only one foot. When your right foot is on the ground, your left foot should be in the air, and when your left foot is in the air, your right foot should be on the ground. However, if the terrain changes, it becomes difficult to apply this technique. But there’s an advantage: because the foot that’s in the air doesn’t bear weight while you walk, you’ll be less tired.

## Tempo and Balance

Don’t push yourself too hard to keep up with others. If you do, you’ll only become more tired. Everyone has their own pace. Stick to your own pace. You will inevitably find someone who matches your rhythm. Pay attention to taking short and balanced steps – this is effective for a safer walk.

## Walking Speed

The average speed without a backpack is around 4.5-5 km/h on smooth terrain. When a backpack is carried, this speed decreases to approximately 3.5 km/h. The terrain conditions also affect the speed. On non-hard, sandy roads, the average speed is around 2.5 km/h, while in dense forests, it drops to as low as 2 km/h. When traveling on mountainous terrain while climbing uphill, the speed is approximately 1.5 km/h.

## Molalar

Avoid taking long breaks. I usually aim for 40–50 minutes of walking followed by a 10-minute break. On a very steep climb, 5 minutes standing still can help you recover. If you sit down on a long break, your body relaxes and it can be difficult to get moving again. On a long route, split the trail into 2 and take one longer break. Eat something that will restore your energy. If there’s a steep climb coming up, leave the meal until afterwards. Take care not to get cold during breaks if you’re sweaty. Trekking poles can help you tire less.

## Guide

Follow your guide exactly as they instruct you. If you don’t have a guide, choose one person from among you to be the guide. If the group is large, you should have two or more guides. One guide should be at the front, and another at the back. If you don’t have a guide, load the route information for the course onto your phone or GPS device before you start. Always keep your life jacket within easy reach.

## Risk Areas

Accidents can happen anywhere, even while trekking. Stay with the group, follow your guide’s instructions and don’t go anywhere without telling someone. Let people know even if you’ll only be gone for 2 minutes. Don’t say I didn’t warn you. We’ve had things happen to us, and I’m speaking from experience. Carry a whistle in case you get lost. If there’s a chance of encountering a wild animal, I suggest making noise as you walk, for example by blowing the whistle to warn it away. Be especially careful on descents and on slippery or rocky ground. Injuries are more common on the way back or near the finish, when you need to pay extra attention.

Finally, don’t compete with nature – you will lose. If your knowledge and experience are insufficient, don’t undertake that task and don’t venture into unknown paths.
