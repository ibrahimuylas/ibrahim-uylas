This is a photographic account of the construction of our handmade Wa'apa Ama kanomuz Plankton, which began on a rooftop in 2013 and concluded in July 2014, reaching the sea.

> Safety notice: This content is not a technical production project or current material standard. Shipbuilding; requires expertise for transport capacity, connections, waterproofing, and navigation safety. Before starting a similar project, use current plans and the advice of a specialist.

## Quick summary

- Two-person Wa'apa canoe
- Beginning: May 2013
- Water landing: July 2014
- Basic materials: Marine plywood, iroko battens, epoxy and polyester resin
- The scope of this text: Rather than technical specifications, it’s a photographic account of the process we’ve experienced, from the model to test drives.

**Name**: Plankton

Birth Date: 2014

Death Date: Winter 2015-2016

**Construction site:** The 9th floor of an apartment block on Çamlıca Hill.

Modeli: Wa’apa Ama

**Parents:** İbrahim Uylaş, Yunus Demiray

In summary;

Plankton; the canoe we began making with our own hands and sweat on our terrace in May 2013, we launched into the water in July 2014. This canoe, which was Yunus’s dream, became like my own child. Sometimes we struggled a lot, sometimes we got bored, but we held on and reached the end.

We would like to express our gratitude to our family, who patiently endured all the noise, dust, and dirt we created, to the neighbors who didn’t complain about our noise, and finally, to our friends who, from the beginning, believed in our success and supported us.

It’s a long post, so I’ve put photos in as I go to show you each step. That way you won’t think I’m talking complete nonsense :)

She has a lot of stories and a rich history. We did something that few people in Turkey know about. We saw people doing waterskiing in Turkey, but we couldn’t find anyone who did canoeing like us. We searched, but we couldn’t find them online, or anywhere else. Let me tell you in detail how we started, and what we experienced during this process. I also plan to share a separate article for those who want to hear about the technical details.

## How did we begin making canoes?

There was a sudden thought in my mind – the idea to build a canoe – which led me into this. Yunus, a man deeply passionate about the sea. His wife’s name is even Deniz. I don’t know which one he was more passionate about, but after he started working at TEB, he really tried to convince me to build a canoe. One day, Yunus was trying to convince me again, and he started by saying, “If you want to build a canoe, you can’t just say ‘let’s build a canoe,’” and that’s where everything began. “Okay, let’s do it,” I thought, and the idea was good, but when I was thinking, “How will this work?” Yunus suddenly presented his plans. “Yes, he has plans, and we’ll do it according to them.”

## Where will we build the canoe?

There’s also another problem: where we would do it. It could be with the dolphins’ apartment garden, but the residents would certainly complain about all the noise. Plus, there’s a risk of theft at night. Since it won’t be finished in a month, we really need to find a place. We thought about renting a shop, but the costs would go up significantly this time. The most logical option seems to be our own house’s terrace. Because there’s renovation work going on in the surrounding buildings, no one would complain about the noise we’re making. The only problem was how we would get things down on the 9th-floor terrace after doing the work. After measuring the gap in the stairs, we decided that the cannon would pass, and we would use ropes in case of any problems.

## What People Say

What are they not saying? Many people think we can't do it, expressing this as “you’ll either finish in one month or won’t be able to/won’t be able to.” People who are honest with us make it clear on their faces. But others show it in their looks. Because we’re accepting this as a reality, we haven’t denied it. Even my brother was thinking, “my brother has really gone crazy.” He said it to my face – he was embarrassed and awful. Generally, people didn’t believe we could succeed, because they thought we were getting involved in something they didn’t understand. But we were thinking, “why shouldn’t it work, if we do it according to the plan.”

## Arge Süreci

It is a venture we knew little about, which is why we carried out approximately one month of research. The first thing that caught our attention was that, although our country is surrounded by sea on three sides, we were largely unfamiliar with such activities. Abroad, many people are involved in canoeing, creating plans, and selling them. You can purchase the plans alone, or buy the canoe as a kit along with the plans. Yes, you are not mistaken – they can even send you the kit, much like IKEA furniture. These people have really expanded the business. We, on the other hand, decided to make the two-person version of the Wa'apa Ama model canoe, which is easy to transport and can be stored at home. [You can access it here](https://web.archive.org/web/20181204163303/http://homepages.paradise.net.nz/garyd/wa_apa.html). We contacted Gary Dierking and asked for a better price on the $150 plans. He informed us that we could purchase the book for $15 and proceed from there. If you search for "[Building Outrigger Sailing Canoes](https://www.amazon.com/Building-Outrigger-Sailing-Canoes-Construction/dp/0071487913)" on Amazon.com, you will find the book. This proved to be a better option for us, as the book provides extensive detail and explains how to build them. It also describes how to construct various models of canoe. Should you be thinking about building a canoe, this book will be of great assistance.

## And we begin – Model Construction

Our first task was to read the initial book. Then, we decided to build a model out of cardboard, reducing the dimensions in the book by a ratio of 1/10. We wanted to see if it actually worked, and also to gain an idea by creating a model before replicating the real thing. Of course, we also began searching for suitable wood types for marine-contras (water-resistant plywood) and trim. We didn’t understand anything from the initial samples, so we spent about 2-3 months on research and model building. Once the model of the canon appeared, we thought, “This is it!” and then we moved on to sourcing materials, contacting suppliers to complete the model.

## Material Procurement

We started by looking for plywood. We told them we were looking for 6mm thick, five-layered plywood that was 2.5 meters long. We also heard, at the places we went to, “It seems you’re new at this, isn’t it?” We decided to go to the place we were going to in a disheveled manner, in a way that suggested we understood the tricks of the trade, so they wouldn’t try to deceive us. We put on our work clothes and went. When we asked if there was any 2.5-meter long plywood, his reaction was, “It seems you’re new at this, isn’t it? This is the first time you’re buying plywood.” “Seriously?” we said, “Let’s see if we can pull off some shady business.” We bought Iroko wood, suitable for use in moldings, in the dimensions we wanted. We took it to the carpenter next to us to have the moldings cut to the desired length – approximately 30*20cm, 3 meters long. The carpenter handled it himself, while we, two people, couldn’t even move it two shops over. “As usual,” I was complaining, “Let him come and type on a 10-finger keyboard.”

The whole thing kicked off then. We were giving the carpenter the measurements, he was cutting, and we were sorting out the resulting pieces. Of course, we’d already worked out the log’s dimensions and had done plenty of drawing and planning to figure out which sizes to cut and how much. There are zillions of different plank sizes. The calculations gave us a proper headache. So, there was no “just chop it off” approach. When I told Yunus, “Look at you, you’re covered in dust,” he replied, “Look after yourself.” That was when we realised we were all covered in dust and that the real work had begun. Iroko wood is so hard it was burning as it was being cut, and it produced a nasty dust that burned your throat. The carpenter certainly didn’t want to cut it. We paid 200 lira for the wood and 150 lira for the cutting :). Our only concern was to use the strongest, best material.

## Fine cuts

Everything started beautifully. We lacked nothing; everything was spot on. First, we built our workbench. Then, we marked out the dimensions for cutting the plywood and made the fine cuts. Since we’d bought a huge sheet of plywood, the marking and cutting went smoothly. That way, there were no deviations in our measurements. Once all the fine cuts were done, millimetre-scale differences appeared between the pieces. We paired up the matching parts and smoothed them with sandpaper – that’s where we first got our hands dirty with sanding. After that, the sanding never seemed to end, and it turned out to be the most exhausting job of all.

## Time to glue it all together

The fine cuts were done and the strips were ready. Next came gluing. We were calmer now, because even holding the plywood up by hand revealed the canoe’s shape, which was hugely satisfying. We started gluing the strips, thinking that once they were glued, we’d be done. The long straight ones were easy to glue. But we didn’t have enough clamps, even with 21, so we could only glue 1 piece a day. It also took 1 day to dry. The curved pieces made it even harder.

## Assembling the Parts

While we’d been gluing the strips, months had gone by. Eventually they were all glued. We thought we’d fit the big sections together like a jigsaw and be done. Then we realised they wouldn’t stick together like that. Glue had squeezed out on the left, the right, everywhere. Epoxy is so strong that once it sets it’s as hard as stone, and we’d been far too generous with it. It has to bond to wood rather than to cured epoxy. What else could we do? Sanding! That blasted job was back, and this time it was worse. Getting epoxy off the smaller, recessed surfaces killed our arms. We had a power sander, but 3 or 4 hours of that vibration left us wrecked. We kept sanding for a long time. When it was finally done, we ruled guide lines on the table and started gluing all the sections together.

## What on earth is that?

Before gluing the hull, we drew guide lines on the table and prepared a mold to ensure it was straight. We brought all the parts and glued half the hull together. We checked the squareness. Surprisingly, it was perfectly straight. How? We didn't understand, so we measured again. When we measured from the right and left, we reached the exact endpoints with no deviation, not even a millimeter. That's it, my friend. We had been getting all high and mighty, thinking we were pros at this. The next day, we were going to take it out of the mold and glue the other half of the hull. And what do we see? The mold was too big, it wouldn't come out. "Oh my!" We didn't understand how that happened. We hadn't accounted for it. So what do we do? While thinking that, we started breaking the mold. "Well, we couldn't break the hull, could we?" :)

## 3mmlik Hata

The hull is finished, and now it's time to attach the side leg. We attached it nicely. That one is also two pieces, just like the hull. But there's a problem. When we bring the two boxes together side by side, a 3mm gap appears. Imagine two cubes: when you put them next to each other, the corners don't stick out, right? But ours has an extra 3mm on one side. "Oh well, let's leave it," I was about to say, "we'll just have a 3mm flaw," but Yunus said no: it would be an obstacle when moving in the water, it would ruin the aerodynamics. I saw the doctor was right. Imagine a canoe that constantly pulls to the right or left. Ahaha, isn't that funny? It's done, but it doesn't go straight, ahaha. Finally, we covered the bottom of the canoe with epoxy over our polyester layer. This made the bottom much more robust. We can now scrape it against rocks and so on, which is necessary.

## Will this canoe float?

The question that kept nagging at us was this. Of course, we hadn’t considered that it would sink and capsize. Everyone thought that, but not us. Because we had used so much adhesive, we felt we needed to wipe off the excess. For a full-sized canoe, about 5kg of epoxy would have been enough, yet we had used nearly 9kg. That was the problem: the iroko wood we’d bought for the planks was already heavy enough, and then we added even more adhesive on top. What would happen? We didn’t know. We were constantly crunching the numbers. Yunus was anxious, while I was, as usual, relaxed. “At the very worst,” I’d say, “it’ll take on water halfway up and still float.” But of course, we couldn’t paddle it. According to our latest calculations, it would float, and so we felt relieved.

## Painting Stages

Yeah, as if we went straight on to painting. No chance… After the gluing, we had to clean off the excess adhesive that had gone everywhere. More sanding, in other words. Damn this job. We couldn’t catch a break. We got it done anyway, excited that we’d finally get to paint. We sanded the whole canoe again and gave the terrace a proper clean. How on earth did we fill 10 rubbish bags? Had we thrown nothing away for 1.5 years? I don’t know, but there were easily 10 bags. We covered everything with plastic sheeting and got to work with our epoxy primer.

## Let this business be finished; the rest will be easy.

Well, we said this right from the start. Once this job was done, the rest would be easy, nah, not easy. It got harder and harder. Even just painting became more difficult. Don’t get me started on painting. Once it’s epoxy-based, it starts drying immediately. Your brush gets stiff and sticky. Yes, this happens about 15 minutes after you start applying the paint. With one coat of epoxy primer and three coats of white epoxy paint, our little one finally appeared. Just sit back and watch, it’s something else. You can’t help but fall in love. White suits it perfectly, you know? :)

## Test Drive

With close friends and curious, helpful compatriots from our surroundings, we conducted the canoe's test run on Caddebostan beach. The screws were too small for the holes, so we hammered them in to get them through. But then, the screws wouldn't come out. Next, we brought a drill, enlarged the holes, and re-inserted the screws, but the nuts wouldn't go on. That's because the screw tips had been damaged when we hammered them. After overcoming these little mishaps, we launched the canoe into the water around 9:30 PM. To make sure nothing happened to anyone, Yunus and I did the first test run. Well, we deserved it at that point. We also had a slight nagging worry: could this thing actually become a submarine? :) The first launch into the water was so beautiful that I was shouting, "I'm swimming, I'm swimming!" It far exceeded our expectations, sinking about four fingers deep, and the water literally flowed out, kid. Wow. It was like having a child. Normally, I wouldn't be getting into a boat and rowing, but I was practically flying with joy. With two oars, we sped up immediately.

## Canoe transport kit

We did a good job, but we also needed to transport it properly in the car. So, we decided to make a car kit. We used thick foam cushions, and Deniz, with his skillful hands, made a leather cover. We threaded the ropes and tied them to the car. It turned out to be sturdier than we expected and didn’t press against the roof. The only problem was lifting the huge canoe (the hull alone weighs about 40kg) and placing it on top of the car. We also made a wheeled wooden platform, and we managed to drag it all the way to the shore. We even took a long trip with the car kit, all the way from İstanbul to Ağva. The only catch is: if it’s too windy, don’t exceed 70 km/h. If there’s no wind, you can cruise comfortably between 90–100 km/h. And if you tie it to the car from the front and back, then it’s simply unbeatable.

## Final Stop

Currently, the kayak is lying in a suitable marina. What will he do during the winter, though. When summer comes, we’ll set off again.
