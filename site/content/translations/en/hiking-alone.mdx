Going trekking alone requires courage. There’s no need to explain the reason in detail – you already know it. Going alone is always riskier. Especially if you are a woman, it’s incredibly difficult, even to imagine. It’s a very sad situation to feel unsafe. As a man, it’s easier to go alone, yes, but there have been times when I’ve felt the same unease. I don’t have a fear of nature, but whenever I see a person, a question mark forms in my mind. It’s not the question of what I’d do if I met a bear; it’s the question of what I’d do if I met someone who could be dangerous – that’s what really makes me think. Of course, I’m not making a generalisation and telling you to avoid every person you see, but when you see what happens, you have to be cautious. When I’m at the top of the mountain, going alone with my backpack, I get uneasy if I see someone – someone whose presence isn’t part of my concept – but that’s not because I’m mad and afraid of every person I see.

That brings me to Adrienne Hall’s book **Solo Hiking**, published in Turkish as *Tek Başına Doğa Yürüyüşü*. I couldn’t find her biography online, so I’m going by the publisher’s introduction. It describes her as a professional hiker living in Alaska who studied biology and went on to do a master’s in environmental studies. Someone who loves the outdoors, like you and me. She’s put her solo walking experience to good use here. Walking alone has its own pleasures and its own risks. Hall starts with how to prepare, goes through camping kit and first aid supplies, and explains how to deal with the difficulties you might run into on your own. The second half is about safety, from injuries to snakebite. One detail stuck with me. If a snake bites you, get emergency help straight away. Don’t try to suck out the venom. It’s an unlikely thing to face, but worth knowing. What I like about the book is how clearly Hall shows what changes when there’s no group around you. If you fancy going solo, I’d give it a place on your shelf.
