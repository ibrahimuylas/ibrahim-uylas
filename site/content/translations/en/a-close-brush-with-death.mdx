---
title: "A Close Brush with Death"
description: "I set off for a hike in Sadağı Canyon. On the road to Bursa, I came out of a bend and saw my whole lane shining with oil."
---
One Saturday morning in the summer of 2016 I loaded up the bike and set off from İstanbul. The plan was to ride to Sadağı Canyon in Orhaneli, go for a day hike, then call in on my aunt’s family in Bursa.

I’ve got a wild streak, but in reality, I’m not a reckless motorcyclist. I don’t speed much, I don’t take risks, and generally, I ride like a gentleman. Strangely, that day I was riding a bit aggressively. Now and then I’d cut in, now and then I’d hammer the throttle, that’s how it went. When I reached the Gemlik area, there’s a winding road climbing uphill from Gemlik towards Bursa. After climbing to the top, you reach a straight road leading to Bursa. It was exactly on that road, just before the final straight, when I was in the leftmost lane of a three-lane road that the devil nudged me. I was in the leftmost lane and there was a right-hand bend. I looked and saw the lanes to my right were empty. The devil whispered to me: if you stick it to the rightmost lane, you can take the bend more easily and pass a whole bunch of cars and trucks. I thought about it, it seemed very logical. I cut across into the rightmost lane, leaned the bike to the right and took the bend. Just as I was coming out of the bend, what did I see? My lane was shining brightly. As I opened the throttle coming out of the bend, while the bike was still at about 60 degrees to the ground, I suddenly found myself on the oil on the road. There was nowhere to escape. The whole lane was already covered in oil, where were you supposed to go? That was the moment time stopped for me. Everything stopped. While the devil was whispering, Azrael was saying “come, come.” In my last breath, curses came out of my mouth instead of a prayer. It wasn’t against anyone, I was just shouting it out into the void. What could I do? How could I tear up the calculations? The rear wheel slipped. I hadn’t straightened the bike properly, so I couldn’t escape to anywhere. My speed was around 80 km/h. The rear wheel slid right, then left, and we kept sliding like that for a while. Meanwhile, I tried to regain control, hoping to manage it with occasional throttle and occasional braking. Of course, it was futile. The last thing I remember is that we were lying parallel to the ground on our left side, yes, the bike and I. If I didn’t let go, the bike would throw me over and come after me; there was no escape. That was the moment we had passed the point of no return. The last breath. I couldn’t even say “Oh God, I’m coming to you.” The whole event happened within 2-3 seconds. At most, 5 seconds. In such situations, they say you should let go of the bike so it doesn’t throw you over and come after you. So there is such theoretical knowledge in my head. To put that theory into practice at that moment, I immediately let go of the bike. The bike went off in front, and I slid behind it. I thought maybe I could catch up, but the bike was moving faster.

I hit the ground hard. Was I rolling? Had I hit something? Was I still in one piece? Then I noticed how blue the sky was. Bob Dylan was playing in my ears and I was sliding down the road on my back. I drew in my arms and legs. I’d tucked my head in too, half by instinct, curling up into a ball. Otherwise my head would have gone bang, bang, bang against the road. I might have broken my neck and never got to write this.

When I stopped sliding, I didn’t get up straight away because I couldn’t tell what was wrong with me. I lifted my head a little and what do you know? A huge lorry was coming straight at me. I let out a second “Oh, shit.” I slid myself over into the hard shoulder. Lan, we’d escaped the crash only to end up under a lorry. What a joke! Luckily, the driver saw me fall and stayed close so cars wouldn’t suddenly overtake him on the left and run me over. Thank goodness, the man ran over right away. He checked my hands, then my feet, then very gently my neck. Nothing. How could that be? I’d expected some kind of injury after that fall, but I seemed as solid as a pig. Or maybe I just didn’t understand. Once I realised my neck was fine, I took off my helmet and stood up. Ulan, I really was fine. I ached all over from the fall, but I was OK. Oh, thank goodness.

I looked for the motorbike. No motorbike. It had rolled down to the side, a bit lower, and disappeared into the grass, barely visible. "All right," I thought, "let it stay here. It’s probably been torn to pieces anyway, no need to drag it away. I saved my life, that’s enough." We went down to take a look. There wasn’t much on it either. Just the crash bars were bent. A man helped us get the motorbike back onto the hard shoulder, and then he carried on, not wanting to cause another accident. While I was checking the motorbike thoroughly, about 20 minutes had passed since the crash when an ambulance arrived. "There’s been an accident here," they said, "we’ve been driving round looking for it." The doctor gave me a quick glance and, seeing I was fine, filled out the ambulance report and carried on. Meanwhile, an older woman passing by gave me a hard time for riding a motorbike. What was she doing on the Bursa–İstanbul highway? Where had she come from, where was she going? There wasn’t even a settlement nearby yet, and I still don’t understand. Just as I’d taken in the warning "Don’t ride those things, it’s dangerous," a pickup truck stopped and a motorbike-riding uncle offered to help me. After I told him there was nothing wrong with the motorbike and that I’d carry on once I’d had a bit of rest and recovered myself, we said our goodbyes to that uncle too.

One last look at the bike. Bent crash bars, broken mirrors, rear luggage mount snapped. That was it. If you’d asked me to crash at 80 km/h and have both rider and bike come out pretty much unscathed, I couldn’t have done better. Maybe a good deed had come back to me. Maybe someone had been praying for me. Either way, thank God. After half an hour’s rest I rode the hard shoulder into Bursa. Only about 10 km to go. I found a mechanic to check the bike, just in case. The hike was off, obviously. I’d nearly died an hour ago. What bloody hike? Congratulations to me on my demonstration of how to crash and barely leave a scratch.

Ultimately, even if you are always careful and aware of your surroundings, you can’t always predict what will happen. Thankfully, I managed to avoid a serious accident and realized once again the importance of the equipment I was wearing.

Here’s to clear roads under our wheels and safe rides for us all.
