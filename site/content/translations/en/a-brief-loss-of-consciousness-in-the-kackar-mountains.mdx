---
title: "A Brief Loss of Consciousness in the Kaçkar Mountains"
description: "On an hour’s sleep, Recep, Enes and I set off for Naletleme Pass. By nightfall we were losing sight of the cairns and starting to lose the plot."
---
August 2013

We left Erzurum at six in the morning on an hour’s sleep and reached the Olgunlar highland in the Kaçkar Mountains around noon. A few clouds, lovely weather, and all we want to do is get walking. We leave the car there and set off for Naletleme Pass. We’ve got three days. Today we’ll cross the pass and camp. Tomorrow we’ll explore the lakes on the far side, visit Yukarı Kavrun and Ayder, and spend the night at Kavrun. On day three we’ll come back over the pass.

The trail to the pass continues as a path through a valley. Thanks to the stream flowing beside us, we drink ice-cold water and don't have to carry any. After walking a bit inside the valley, the summit of Mount Kaçkar and Naletleme Pass come into view. The snow and clouds on the mountain top add excitement to us. When we reach the entrance of the pass, we've been walking for about four hours. Everywhere at the pass is rocky, and because of the clouds, you can't see how the trail goes. As a team, we're exhausted: Recep, Enes and I. One hour of sleep at night, six hours of car travel, and about four hours of path climbing with 15–16 kg bags on our backs. It would be nice to stay somewhere here, but our plans would be derailed. What's going to happen? We think, "We'll walk another three hours, cross the pass, and set up camp by the lake around seven," so we plunge into the pass. Visibility is about 10 metres; due to the mist, we can only see the rocks and stones. There's no path. There are only stacked stones (babas). We continue moving by following them. The elevation keeps rising and the air gets colder. On the 15th of August, we're shivering because there's snow beside us. We put on another layer of clothes and continue on the way. There isn't a single living thing around. No bird song, no wind, no movement at all.

We’re wandering around at about 3,200 metres. Wandering? We can barely lift an arm from exhaustion. We’re looking for a bit of flat, earthy ground to pitch the tent, but there’s nothing. That’s when we understood the origin of this pass’s name. It’s nearly eight and the light is fading. We take out the torches and carry on. Everything’s fine, except for the fog. Without it, we’d at least know where we are. I check the GPS, but it makes no sense. We’re somewhere near a lake, but how do we get there? We don’t know. Because everywhere is rocky and stony. To top it all, none of us has trekking boots. We’ve come wearing trainers. We do fitness in the gym, right? What’s the point of taking and wearing trekking boots? We feel every single stone on the ground. Down to the tiniest little curve :)

No birds fly through here, nobody passes this way, nothing seems to live here, and we can’t find a way out. If a wolf stepped out of this fog now, I wouldn’t even be surprised. Just what we need. Enes blanks for a moment and mistakes me for a friend of his. What’s he doing here? Where are we? His legs keep going all the same. Then I move ahead of Recep. Now it’s Recep’s turn. Who’s that up front? Is there someone else with us? He’s having this conversation in his head, so the rest of us have no idea. Two minutes later I lose sight of the cairns. Oh, brilliant. We’re losing the plot. The GPS is sod all use. We’re close to the lake, but one fall on these rocks in the dark and that’ll be it. God, I think we’re coming to meet you. If we could find a bit of level ground we’d lie down and sleep. There’s no earth anywhere. Our feet alone would celebrate the feel of it. We stop and I look around. We’re dead on our feet. Our choices seem to be to roll out our sleeping bags on the trail or rest for two hours and then carry on. While I’m looking for the cairns I spot a patch of green just big enough for the tent, if you squint. By green I mean bushes 30–40 cm high. We pitch the tent right on top. After a bite to eat I deliver these words for posterity. “Let’s just sleep. In the morning we can decide whether to go on or turn back. Right now, let’s sleep.”

We’d put our lives at risk getting up there. Thirteen hours of unbroken sleep later, we woke to sunshine. From there on we actually enjoyed the trip. Amazing what a rest and a bit of sun can do.

My notes to myself after that one? Get some sleep before you set off. If you’re behind schedule, let the schedule go. Wear your hiking boots. When you’re tired, stop. And for goodness’ sake, man, behave yourself :)
